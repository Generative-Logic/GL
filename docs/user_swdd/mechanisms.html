4 · Proof mechanisms

How the engine actually reasons. Five mechanisms compose on the substrate; §4.2 is the core, traced end to end on a real Gauss theorem.

4.1  Disintegration

Disintegration expands a compound so its pieces can fire rules. Two paths.

4.1.1  Pass A — structural

disintegrateExprCore2 splits an and / existence into its element expressions and recurses. No fresh variables, nothing to gate.

4.1.2  Pass B — operator-aware

disintegrateExpr2 mints fresh iteration variables — it_… (algebra) or int_… (integration) — to stand for unknowns. A fresh variable may not enter the result until admitted (§4.2). Pass B fires only when !parameters.compressor_mode && !parameters.ban_disintegration (I-7) — the single flag also gates back-reformulation and hypothetical disintegration. Its freshness check rides savedStartInt — a counter that must stay monotonic; a parallel counter breaks it (I-17).

4.2  Admission, rejection & equivalence classes

One mechanism, three faces. Admission decides whether a fresh variable may be emitted; rejection parks the ones that can't yet; equivalence classes rewrite parked work into a form that can. The loop they form is why a single prove pass is enough.

Admission updates are drained once per burst. The admission-map writes a hash burst produces — on the algebra side a fresh template, its not-yet-fired status, and the revival of any parked rejection the new template now admits; on the integration side the fresh integration template the burst registers — are buffered as they are discovered and replayed once, in that order, after the burst's evaluation pass, rather than mutating the admission containers inline during it. The pass stays free of admission-container mutation, giving the forthcoming static-memory (ASIC) build a single well-defined drain point. Admission decisions, the proved theorems, and every proof are unchanged.

4.2.1  The two admission paths

A Pass-B fire that mints a fresh variable is gated by two paths, first-match-wins:

  • Map-based — authoritative. isAdmitted consults admissionMap (registered consumer-side by the integration handshake, §4.3). The int_ path uses isAdmittedIntegration vs admissionMapIntegration, then the admissionSetIntegration fallback.
  • Single-input-operator standalone. isAllowedAsOperatorInput admits iff the core is in operators and the operator has exactly one input arg (in practice in, in2) and the var sits in that slot. Never widen this (I-6). Widening to multi-input operators broke Gauss summation; the standalone never consults the admission map.

Fail both → the variable is buffered in pendingRejections (algebra) / pendingRejectionsIntegration (integration). A cascade-admission loop retries; if it still can't admit, the rejection is committed (§4.2.2).

4.2.2  Rejected maps

A failed fresh variable is never silently dropped — a lost fact is unsound silence (I-19). It is committed to rejectedMap (algebra, it_) or rejectedMapIntegration (integration, int_), keyed by the marked form (the constituent with its output arg replaced by the token "marker"). The integration value keeps everything needed to replay the fire verbatim later:

4.2.3  Five maps, three key shapes

The admission/rejection machinery spans five maps in three key shapes. They are not interchangeable — a lookup in the wrong shape silently misses, and the revival mechanism then “fires” but accomplishes nothing. Always transform at the boundary.

  • Mnemonic. u_ = universal template; bare = concrete runtime instance. Admission MAPS store templates; the admission SET and both rejected MAPS store concrete instances.

4.2.4  Equivalence classes

When (=[a,b]) is admitted, a and b join an EquivalenceClass at the current scope. The class is owned per-validityName (Memory::equivalenceClassesMap).

EquivalenceClassHolds
memberIdsthe known-equal members
intEqualityLevelsMapper-pair admission levels (for level gating)
equalityOriginMapprovenance — which equalities formed the class (the merge logic's source of truth)
  • Forming. New pair → new class; one side known → extend; both known → merge.
  • Mirror & transitivity. (=[b,a]) emitted automatically when a≠b (I-9); transitivity is class membership, not a derived rule.
  • Negated equality, one-sided. !(=[a,b]) expands to sibling inequalities on each side separately; the full cross-product is deliberately not emitted (combinatorial, no new content). The expansion fires both when the negation arrives and when a class touching either argument forms or grows later — insertion order does not matter. The recorded proof step cites the justifying equality at the scope where that equality's own history entry lives (the class's scope or an ancestor of it), never at the rewritten negation's scope — inside an or-branch the branch-scope form would be unresolvable for the proof-graph walker, whose ancestor lift deliberately stops at branch boundaries.
  • Scoped & additive. A class at scope S is visible at every descendant. Cross-scope application deposits at deeperOf(class, expr) and never overwrites the ancestor original; cross-scope merge copies ancestor members in but leaves the ancestor class read-only (I-31).

4.2.5  The revival loop — a Gauss trace

A parked rejection revives two ways: an equivalence-class rewrite (applyEquivalenceClassToRejectedMapIntegration) turns its variables into a shape that now matches an admission entry, or a new admission key arrives (revisitRejectedIntegration2) that matches the parked key. Either way the rewritten constituent (plus siblings) is emitted to sameIterationInternalMail and absorbed at the top of the next cycle with full re-expansion (status=1, not the legacy mail's status=3). Asymmetries vs the algebra side: integration revival does not call cleanAdmissionMap (the rule stays live for future matches — I-22) and does not cascade through addExprToMemoryBlock (linear path only).

Worked trace — a Gauss revival, end to end

Theorem sequence[1,4,2,X,Y] → sequence[1,4,2,9,11] under limitSequence ∧ in2[9,12,3]. Anchor mapping puts 4=+.

  1. Cycle N — reject. A compound sequence[1,4,2,X,Y] lands at v=main. Pass B unfolds it and mints integration witness int_lev_2_42 for an inner existential. One body element is (in3[a,int_lev_2_42,b,4]). Both admission probes miss (no template for these concrete a,b). It's buffered; the cascade can't admit; committed:
    rejectedMapIntegration[(in3[a,marker,b,4])@main] = { concrete (in3[a,int_lev_2_42,b,4]), siblings [(in[int_lev_2_42,1])], compound sequence[1,4,2,X,Y] }. Nothing visible was added — the proof is stuck on this rung.
  2. Cycle N+k — equi rewrite. Another block proves (=[X,9]). Class {X,9} forms at v=main. applyEquivalenceClassToRejectedMapIntegration fires: the rmi key contains a=X; rewrite → (in3[9,marker,b,4]); probe admissionMapIntegration[(in3[u_9,marker,u_b,u_4])] → MATCH (an earlier compound's integration template registered it). Emit to sameIterationInternalMail: primary (in3[9,int_lev_2_42,b,4]) and sibling (in[int_lev_2_42,1]), each with an equality1 origin [pre, (=[a,9])]. Erase the rmi entry; leave the admission entry.
  3. Cycle N+k+1 — absorb & prove. Top-of-burst absorb at status=1 → disintegrateExpr2 runs, the two atoms land at v=main, updateAdmissionMapIntegration fires on each, downstream integration picks them up, the enclosing sequence→sequence implication's premise chain is now satisfied, head sequence[1,4,2,9,11] derives and moves to theorems.txt.
  4. What the verifier re-checks. The chapter row for (in3[9,int_lev_2_42,b,4]) is tagged equality1 with source (in3[a,int_lev_2_42,b,4]) and equality (=[a,9]): same core, arity 4, the one differing arg (position 0) is covered by the supplied equality → row passes (Chapter 7).

4.3  Integration

Integration is disintegration run backwards: reassemble a compound from its constituents so the whole can be asserted as a conclusion. The two are a matched pair — the consumer-side output-slot registration integration performs (updateAdmissionMap) is exactly what populates the admission maps disintegration consults (§4.2.1). Reassembly uses expandSignatureForIntegration / buildIntegrationInstruction (both via the single binder rule, I-4) and is staged in a scoped child block: each step emits a validity name row (the scope it's bound to) and an expansion for integration row (the re-expansion); on completion it commits to the parent as one step. A staged integration that can't finish yet rides the same sameIterationInternalMail revival channel as a parked rejection. Three preparatory reformulation tags appear: … and, … >[bound] (carrying the bound variable), … >[] (witness slot already filled).

4.4  Branching & contradiction

Case analysis is the hardest thing GL does, and every FTA-ladder rung rests on it. An or<N> is a compiled disjunction (De Morgan: E₁∨E₂ = !(&!E₁!E₂)).

4.4.1  Splitting an OR

Splitting first treats every contiguous nested OR as one logical level: (D₁∨D₂)∨D₃ becomes the ordered leaf cohort D₁, D₂, D₃ in the same cycle. The original outer compiled OR remains the branch, provenance, and bookkeeping identity. Splitting then does two things at once:

  • 1a · K mutual-exclusion implications, at the parent scope. For each disjunct D_i, the rule “if every other disjunct is false then D_i” (K=2 collapses to !A→B / !B→A). Unconditional, and its disintegration origin is recorded above the admission gate so the OR stays auditable even if 1b is skipped.
  • 1b · one branch scope per disjunct. encodePush(…, "ordis_…"), seeded with D_i asserted. The other disjuncts' negations are not seeded in _ordis_; the parent-scope rules from 1a remain inherited. Gated by the OR nesting cap (max_or_depth — branch scopes never nest beyond it; the 1a rules still emit at any depth) and by the OR-admission check: allowOrDisintegration, fed from LocalMemoryValue::productOfDisintegration — set iff the firing rule itself is a product of disintegration (a premise carries a u_ arg). Anchor-bound theorems never trigger it; this is the narrowed form after D-31's broad version exploded.
Nested ORs open in one go. A compiled OR child is never stored as an opaque branch in either direction. Both _ordis_ and _orint_ recursively expand OR children until the leaves are non-OR expressions, preserve their order, and keep every leaf under the outer OR signature. Flattening stops at any non-OR operator, so it changes only disjunction syntax, not other definitions.

1c · The introduction half. The K rules of 1a consume a disjunction that is already known; nothing above produces one from a single true disjunct. That half emits at a different site: when the prover installs a compiled implication whose premise is a disjunction (for example an enumeration description's membership-intro conjunct “x=a ∨ x=b ∨ x=c ⇒ x∈M”), it also installs one intro rule per flattened leaf, D_k → (D₁∨…∨D_K) — propositionally valid unconditionally, a flat rule with no scopes. A single concretely-true disjunct — typically reached through an equality witness alias, since the true disjunct at a concrete element is reflexive — can then state the disjunction as a fact and fire the implication's own rule. This closed the rung-2.1 membership gap: the enumerated set's concrete memberships were underivable because the disjunction statement at a concrete element had no producer.

4.4.2  Convergence

Branches recombine in one of two shapes, detected by classifyOrScope:

_orint_ — one branch proves_ordis_ — all converge
A single branch proves the goal's head. The proof may already exist at any ancestor of the goal; the shallowest known source supplies levels and provenance without being copied into the branch. The OR is emitted at the exact goal's parent scope — computed by peeling the last _boundary_ segment off the goal name (not blindly main). cleanUpOrIntegrationBranches wipes only direct integration children with that exact parent and outer signature; equal signatures under another parent remain live.Every branch independently reaches the same C. The cohort is the exact pair (parent, original OR signature). ordisMerge books each deposit in orBookkeeping[(C, cohort)] → set<branch>; when the count hits orDisjunctCount[cohort], C is promoted to that parent via sameIterationInternalMail and only the converged copy collapsed. Equal signatures under different parents never share branches; each cohort stays live for further convergences. A branch whose asserted disjunct is refuted at an ancestor is a dead branch — everything it derives is ex falso — and is retired at the end of the burst: subtree wiped, its entry dropped from every booked run, the cohort count reduced, and convergence re-checked over the survivors. A convergence of the shrunk cohort still accounts for every branch in the exported proof: each retired branch contributes its refutation as a reductio ingredient instead of a branch derivation, and the verifier checks both forms.

4.4.3  Full contradiction discharge

A full contradiction belongs to the current logic block, not to one conditional OR branch. The prover discharges only when both X and !X exist at that block's exact main scope. A copy may also exist deeper; the separate main row still qualifies. primedForContradiction keeps the block alive long enough to emit its stored contradictionTheoremId. The discharge fires at most once per block per step, gated on isActive (I-78).

A block with no special role that reaches such a pair has proved its own premise set inconsistent: it is flagged and deactivated together with its whole subtree, every theorem produced at or below it is suppressed at emission, and rows it already submitted in earlier cycles are retracted from the global theorem list — nothing vacuous ships. After each batch a vacuity pass finishes the job: theorems whose premise sets are certified unsatisfiable (two proved theorems with the same premises and contradictory heads), and theorems whose proofs cite any vacuous theorem, are classified out of the deliverable pool into a dedicated artifact — their chapters stay for audit, but they never feed a later batch. A genuine reductio is unaffected, because only the contradiction child holds the assumed seed, so its parent never flags. The same pair at an anchor block stops the run: it would mean the definition set itself is inconsistent.

4.4.4  OR-theorem construction

A theorem whose head is an OR is not proved directly. After the prove pass, headSwitchOne populates orPairsFromHeadSwitch; run_modes::fullRun pairs mutually-exclusive parents (!D_a→D_b with !D_b→D_a) and constructOrTheorem assembles the or<N> theorem. The disjunct set is canonicalised (min,max) to dedup the symmetric pair; the two parents are marked consumed and moved to compressed_out_theorems.txt — the OR subsumes them.

4.5  Induction

Induction is scheduled on a structurally-identified (1)-typed bound variable. Three sub-goal blocks are spawned: base (n := 0), step (assume the head for n, prove for s(n)), and typing (prove (in[n,N]) first, with typingProofOnly set so induction can't re-enter). The typing block is mandatory: without it, induction would range over all entities, not the naturals — unsound (I-18). All three must land before the theorem is promoted with method = induction (induction variable in the reference column); the chapter is the triad check_zero + check_induction_condition + induction_typing.

4.6  The 5-batch fan-out, in depth

Substrate §3.2.4 lists the five request-generation batches in summary. This section walks each one with the question it answers and the concrete pool it draws from. Reading this end-to-end is the single biggest win for understanding what one cycle actually does inside a logic block.

#Question the batch answersRule poolMandatory-fact source
1“Did the rules I already know fire against the facts I already know?”workingMemory — rules recovered from external mail this burst; emptied and refilled every burst.This LB's full local facts (cumulative).
2“Did the rules I already know fire against the facts I just produced this cycle?”overallHashMemory — rules inherited from ancestors plus everything this LB has ever installed.localEncodedStatementsDelta — this cycle's new facts only.
3“Did any pair-shaped rule fire on a mix of my facts and mail?”Pair-matching rules (two-premise hash chains).Cartesian: local facts × incoming mail.
4“Did the rules I own (not ancestral) fire on every fact the mail brought, broadly?”This LB's local rules (localHashMemory).All mail at v=main, with the locality gate relaxed — the rule is allowed to fire even if no local fact is among the matched premises.
5“Did the rules I just installed this cycle fire on every visible fact, broadly?”Fresh local rules: localHashMemoryDelta.Every visible fact — full Cartesian over the cumulative local + visible-ancestor facts, again with the locality gate relaxed.

4.6.1  Why batches 4 and 5 widen the gate

The locality gate — “at least one matched premise must be a fact this LB owns locally” — is a defence against the engine drowning in cross-product work. But when an ancestor scope mails in a new fact, or a freshly-installed local rule pairs against pre-existing inherited facts, the locality gate would silently withhold the obvious fire. Batches 4 and 5 are the exceptions: they relax the gate precisely once per cycle, and they cap the input pool to keep the relaxation cheap.

4.6.2  What happens if batch 5 is missing

A rule installed in cycle N would not see ancestor-inherited facts until cycle N+1 (batch 1's locality requirement misses the rule's freshness). The practical effect: a one-cycle delay across every rule installation. For a deep induction triad that installs and consumes rules in sequence, the delay multiplies — the run effectively becomes deeper × deeper. Batch 5 collapses that to one cycle.

4.7  The pending-rejection cascade

A Pass-B mint that fails its admission probe is not immediately committed to rejectedMap; first the prover tries a cascade. The buffered variable is held in pendingRejections (or pendingRejectionsIntegration on the integration side), and the cascade re-runs the admission probe each time the admission map gains a new entry that might match. Only if the cascade exhausts without success is the rejection committed.

4.7.1  Why the cascade matters for the single-pass model

Before the cascade existed, a Pass-B variable that couldn't admit on first probe was committed to rejectedMap immediately. If an admission arriving later in the same cycle would have admitted it, the rejection was wasted work — the prover would revive it via revisitRejected2 only on the next cycle. The cascade collapses this latency: every admission arrival inside one cycle revisits the pending buffer, and only on cycle end (when no new admissions can arrive at the same LB) does the residual buffer commit to the rejected map.

The interplay with the revival loop. Once committed, rejectedMap is what the revival loop in §4.2.5 reads. The cascade is the “try harder before committing”; the revival loop is the “try harder after committing.” Together they ensure no admission opportunity is silently lost.
