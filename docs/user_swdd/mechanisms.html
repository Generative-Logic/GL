4.1  Disintegration

Disintegration expands a compound so its pieces can fire rules. Two paths.

4.1.1  Pass A — structural

disintegrateExprCore2 splits an and / existence into its element expressions and recurses. Existence witnesses are temporary until admission. A negated existence !(existence<N>[…]) — “no witness exists” — splits into its existence implications (compiled once on the existence's registry row as compact implication<N>[u_…] atoms) — but only the body direction body → !typing is instantiated: it fires where the body is known and refutes the witness's typing; the typing direction typing → !body, which instantiated the negated body once per typed term and was never a premise, is skipped at the door — and only when both elements are named operators — the Peano predecessor existence over in / in2, say, whose negation is refuted through exactly those rules. An existence with a numbered element — a nested existence<N>, a generated and<N> — has no implications, and its negation is a stored statement only: with them, the typing-premise rules instantiated the negated body over every typed term at every quantifier level (a negated triple existence became a cube of negated conjunctions on typing evidence alone).

4.1.2  Temporary expansion and permanent admission

A failed universal implication with exactly one bound variable supplies a counterexample: every premise holds and its conclusion is false, all for the same witness. The negative compact remains the source. It admits one int_<depth>_lev_… witness without demand when its assigned depth is within the configured limit, its source has no algebraic witness input, and any enclosing witness is admitted. Source-and-scope receipts prevent repeated creation. Nested ordinary witnesses still require admission, and purity is unchanged. Zero- and multiple-witness negative implications remain stored facts.

A negated disjunction is a conjunction of the complemented leaves: NOT (A OR B) gives NOT A and NOT B. Disintegration expands it without OR admission or a case branch, cancels double negation, and preserves the source scope and proof history. This structural step introduces no witness; witness-bearing children still use their own admission.

The prover first expands an existence with reusable temporary placeholders. Admission checks the same algebra, integration and single-input rules and budgets. Only accepted witnesses receive permanent numbers. Read-only admission checks inspect the required fields directly; update payloads are copied only when applying successful admission. An inner witness admitted through a goal's integration also admits an enclosing witness only when that witness has a place in the goal at or above the matched part, or in the existence right beside it; otherwise the inner witness waits until its enclosing witness is admitted. An inner witness admitted through a double-marker demand brings its enclosing witnesses along when the facts of the construction link them to it: for a remainder, the innermost r + p = b names r, and the facts that construct r name the other witnesses. An algebraic or demand-set admission carries no enclosing witness along. A witness admitted through a goal's integration demand fills the instructions waiting for it once, at its admission; afterwards no statement places it, or a name equal to it, into a demand again, so one object never takes two roles. The accepted structure is then published with its full history. An OR cannot open or park inside an unresolved existence.

Shared instructions during witness discovery

Witness paths read a shared prepared instruction. Each path carries its own substitutions and constructs the fields it visits. A consumer that extends the instruction or needs a concrete OR graph materializes that path's view.

Facts a witness brings with it

Some existences hold rules about their own witnesses (see the disintegration package in chapter 8). When the prover expands such an existence, a witness's facts are its own definition followed by the results of the package for it. For the least divisor p of n, these are 2 ≤ p, p ≤ n and p divides n. The results unfold in the same temporary expansion: "p divides n" introduces a new witness d with p · d = n. They are checked for admission like every other fact, and nothing gets a permanent name unless it is admitted. When an admitted path is published, the prover fires the rule again on the actual facts and records each result with the rule and the fact it used, like an ordinary rule firing.

A witness enters the instructions once

When an admission writes a witness into an integration instruction, the instructions take the witness at that moment, and only then. A fact about the witness that arrives later never puts it, or a name proved equal to it, into an open place of an instruction. Without this rule, a prefix admitted for one sequence came back as the running product of the same fold: a swap of roles that no proof uses, and that kept its block busy.

Lookups for replay and cleanup

Demand keys are grouped by their marked expression. Completed constructions are grouped by source and scope. Each lookup finds the affected entries; ordinary admission and completion checks still decide what happens.

A common case is still executed once when it comes from a conjecture premise. Its first execution retains forced witness opening; waiting cohorts reuse the completed result and receive their own case proof. A cohort below an unfired induction scope continues to wait for that scope to settle, unless a missing premise of the induction hypothesis opened it: its cases are released at once, because the hypothesis fires in them.

4.2  Admission, rejection & equivalence classes

One mechanism, three faces. Admission decides whether a fresh variable may be emitted; rejection parks the ones that can't yet; equivalence classes rewrite parked work into a form that can. The loop they form is why a single prove pass is enough.

Admission updates are drained once per burst. The admission-map writes a hash burst produces — on the algebra side a fresh template, its not-yet-fired status, and the revival of any parked rejection the new template now admits; on the integration side the fresh integration template the burst registers — are buffered as they are discovered and replayed once, in that order, after the burst's evaluation pass, rather than mutating the admission containers inline during it. The pass stays free of admission-container mutation, giving the forthcoming static-memory (ASIC) build a single well-defined drain point. Admission decisions, the proved theorems, and every proof are unchanged.

Integration marker keys exclude relations with two inputs and no output. Positive equality is the exception. Typing (in) in both polarities and negative equality remain excluded. The same rule governs key creation, admission lookup and replay from known facts. Unary predicates such as prime, other input counts, and predicates with outputs such as in2 and in3 retain their other eligibility rules.

Excluded expressions remain signed constituents and required construction premises. Thus an order bound alone cannot bind a factor through a marker key, but deriving the factorization still requires all its bounds.

A unique value a premise already names is not integrated. An integration that would build one value, such as the sequence in primeFactorSeq(j, marker, d), is not prepared when that value is marked unique in the configuration and a premise of the block already states the same expression with the other arguments equal, equivalence classes included. Any value the integration could build would equal the one the premise names.

Recursive replay preserves sibling instructions. Each loop visits its original instruction list. Before recording a consumed value, it reloads the current stored map, so instructions added by the preceding replay survive. Those additions are available for later matches.

Admission decisions and key construction have their own interfaces

Rule installation asks AdmissionPolicy whether an existing admission route qualifies. It reads definitions and parameters without changing them. Installation and request generation ask HashKeyBuilder to construct integer keys. The builder keeps its scratch arrays and counters private; a prefix checkpoint lets it try another candidate while preserving earlier variable assignments.

AdmissionMarkerBuilder owns marker generation: it asks the policy which premises qualify, constructs the derivative keys and requests index writes. HashRuleIndex owns insertion, removal and staged flush operations. Its private merge helpers preserve identifier order and ordered unique owner runs; the logical block continues to own the four hash stores and their arenas.

ImplicationMultiplier prepares the ordered binding copies of an implication before installation. It asks BindingPartitions for the permitted variable groupings, retains the existing anchor restrictions and emits copy references into the caller's scratch storage. The component reads the current settings; it does not own the Prover or change configuration.

HashRuleClassifier identifies OR-introduction and subset-exclusion rules and enumerates their instantiated leaves. Its only shared input is the compiled-definition registry. Logical negation is a shared expression operation: removing a leading negation returns an input slice, and adding it uses caller-owned scratch storage.

One logical-block interface handles registration. UnaryRuleMemory registers unary sources and enrolls eligible consumers for existing demands. It operates on the supplied block; composition drains keep their current places in the proof cycle.

Canonicalizer builds the complete equality-class view of a scope from local classes and the parent view. It selects representatives, retains equality history and requests the same dependent rechecks. Temporary rebuilt runs are held in CanonicalRunOverlay, whose record columns and scope index are private; consumers read runs through explicit operations.

CanonicalTemplateMatcher answers canonical admission and source queries using the complete view of the requested scope. It owns template projection and matching, calls Canonicalizer for scope preparation and leaves all source records and comparison indices on the logical block. Shared chain-rendering operations retain their ordering and scratch lifetimes.

The canonicalizer also constructs canonical expression views. It returns the rewritten text and the equality justifications actually used, including whether those equalities are local. Membership lookup and token resolution are private helpers. The result and token records retain their existing caller-owned lifetimes.

RejectedSourceMemory registers parked sources, maintains their canonical lookup records, tracks pending and completed existence work and retires completed parked sources. It uses the canonicalizer and template matcher while the logical block retains the records. Internal receipt and index-maintenance helpers are private; registration, rewrite and retirement order are unchanged.

IntegrationInstructionBuilder prepares integration signatures and instructions from compiled definitions. It keeps history expressions paired with hash-matching expressions, retains binder treatment and uses the same caller arenas and block naming counters. The integration operation still decides when to emit variants and install their rules.

The existing qualification conditions and integer-key format remain the contracts used by hash storage and request evaluation.

4.2.1  The two admission paths

Repeated fixed arguments can request a witness. An interval whose lower and upper endpoints are both zero can supply a demand for its interval set. Creating this demand still requires an eligible predicate and exactly one unfilled argument occurrence. Equality of already fixed arguments is allowed, both when the demand is created and when equal names are later canonicalized.

The five-premise exception is not a theorem-length shortcut. For ordinary output-slot demands, the arithmetic route requires more than the configured number of positive in3 premises and an in3 conclusion. The separate long-rule exception applies only to a product of disintegration with no anchor and at least five premises (the configured threshold). An argument-position u_ prefix identifies such a disintegration product. An anchored order theorem cannot qualify merely because its anchor brings the premise count to five. The independent local-template criterion and other demand routes retain their own checks.

Three operators and a relation can demand the one missing operator value. A rule with more than the configured number of positive in3 premises and at least one relation premise (a compound predicate such as bezEq) also installs output-slot demands, whatever its conclusion. Only an in3 premise can be the missing one, and only if all of its arguments but one are fixed by the other premises, anchor included: its output occurs in no other premise but does occur in the conclusion. When the other premises match, the demand asks for that operator value on the fixed inputs, for example the product q·y that the Bezout transport needs before it can form q·y + x.

Four operators, two of them compound, can demand the missing compound value. A rule with exactly four operator premises, exactly two of them compound definitions with an output (such as appending to a sequence, or folding a sequence into its product), and a compound conclusion, installs an output-slot demand for a compound premise whose output occurs in no other premise but does occur in the conclusion, and whose other arguments the other premises fix. That premise must be the only such one in the rule: a rule with two missing compound values does not demand either of them. When the other premises match, the demand asks for that compound value, for example the sequence obtained by appending one entry, which is then admitted like any operator value. Appending to a sequence is configured as an operator for this in the second FTA batch.

The strong induction step demands the successor of a number it was handed. In the strong step block, a witness of a forced disintegration whose type is a number (for example the length of the factorization the induction hypothesis gives for a smaller number) gets a demand for its successor, filed at the scope where the witness appeared. Extending what the hypothesis delivered, such as appending one more entry to a sequence, needs that next index; the successor is part of the induction apparatus, so no lemma has to ask for it. It also counts as a product of the induction: a rule that uses it may still demand a new construction, which a rule matched on an ordinary algebraic witness may not.

A goal that misses one premise asks the rules that could supply it. When a goal's flattened integration rule is installed, each compound premise that the other premises fix, with at least four other premises beside it, gets a near-miss watch. When the other premises hold somewhere and that one does not, the missing expression goes to a rule search instead of the case-split demands. Every rule that could conclude it is bound to it; for each operator premise of that rule whose output the conclusion leaves open, and whose other arguments at least four other premises fix, a marked implication is installed where the near-miss happened: those premises imply the operator with its output marked. When it fires, it is the ordinary demand for that operator value, which wakes a parked existence of it. The watch and the marked implication are both exempt from the purity rule, because the goal's own witnesses feed them. In the second FTA batch this closes the case 2 ≤ d of the strong step: the goal misses that the appended sequence is ordered; the rule that would give it misses the entry y = F(j); the marked implication demands it, F(j)'s parked existence supplies it, and the least-divisor rule, the ordering rule and the goal's rule follow.

A Pass-B fire that mints a fresh variable is gated by two paths, first-match-wins:

  • Map-based — authoritative. isAdmitted consults admissionMap (registered consumer-side by the integration handshake, §4.3). The int_ path uses isAdmittedIntegration vs admissionMapIntegration, then the admissionSetIntegration fallback, then the double-marker demands: when a flattened goal contains a disjunction whose disjunct has two open arguments, the goal files that disjunct with both open places marked; a construction whose expression has one open place matches it by choosing one more argument as the second marked place, at its scope or any enclosing scope. Such a match admits an ordinary int_ witness. It is also the one route that may build over a single algebra witness.
  • Single-input-operator standalone. isAllowedAsOperatorInput admits iff the core is in operators and the operator has exactly one input arg (today in2, fold, nontrivialFactor), the var sits in that slot, and every parameter of the operator (each argument outside the input and output slots, such as the function in in2) is an original variable, never a constructed function, witness or copy. A preimage is therefore minted under the successor or a premise sequence, but not under a sequence built during the proof. Never widen this (I-6). Widening to multi-input operators broke Gauss summation; the standalone never consults the admission map.

The map-based path has two demand producers. Besides the classic output-slot markers, a premise-rich rule (four or more substantive premises) whose premise carries a variable at an operator input slot that also feeds the rule's head installs an input-slot demand key: when the other premises match live facts, the instantiated key demands exactly the witness shape an existence's disintegration would otherwise park. Because a fired key lands at the deepest scope of its matched premises while the disintegration may run inside a case-split branch, the Pass-B probe checks the deposit scope and its ancestor scopes (never siblings). This is what lets an order fact like x ≤ y surrender its additive witness inside an or-branch — the C8 difference-closure chain. A second, structural producer serves short consumers that the premise-count rule excludes: a received theorem whose only operator premise sits next to compound relations (an order or divisibility fact) installs an input-slot demand key for that premise as long as at least two of its other arguments are bound by those relations — the shape of a < b ∧ a + k = b ⇒ k ≠ 0, which is how the D2 uniqueness case split obtains its difference witness. Successor-witness shapes (one bound argument) stay excluded so that not every ordered term demands a predecessor.

An induction hypothesis can also need an object before its later premises can bind. Inside a recursion step, a positive premise of the step's own chain becomes a demand when it lacks exactly one argument: an operator missing its result, or a defined predicate such as “Q is a sequence on [0, m]” missing Q. The missing object is prepared for construction, not only admission. The demand wakes the parked existence that supplies it (for reverse F3, the restriction of the longer sequence to [0, m]). Two-place relations such as the orders, negated premises and every chain outside a recursion step stay undemanded: a relation demand re-instantiated for every arriving witness is exactly the witness pump the marker route avoids.

The recursion step then advances its hypothesis as facts arrive. An arriving operator fact binds its result; an arriving predicate fact binds whichever argument a demand marks, so the woken restriction re-keys the hypothesis with itself. Some arrivals never advance the hypothesis: a fact produced by unfolding an expression that has a constructed argument, a construction of the hypothesis's own conclusion, and a predicate fact binding an object that the recursion step built over constructed objects, such as the accumulator of a fold over a restriction. An operator fact's result is never refused for that last reason. In the re-keyed hypothesis, each premise that now lacks exactly one argument becomes a new demand, again prepared for construction when it is positive: for reverse F3, the restriction's value at m and its folds up to the induction variable and up to m.

A negated premise waits for the positive ones. A premise that the hypothesis leaves with every argument bound is prepared for proof at once. A negated one, such as "rec is not prime", is proved by a reductio: a scope that assumes "rec is prime" and waits for a contradiction. The hypothesis fires only when all its premises hold, so that scope opens only once the hypothesis's positive premises with every argument bound are known; until then it waits under the first one still missing and opens when that one arrives. For "n ≥ 2 and n not prime ⇒ n has a nontrivial factor" the reductio no longer opens, because "2 ≤ rec" is never known in that step.

A missing premise of the hypothesis opens the OR it belongs to. When every argument of a premise of the induction hypothesis is fixed (at the start of the step its fixed arguments, inside an induction scope also the values the scope's eats filled) and the premise is not known there, the hypothesis waits for exactly that statement. If the statement is one part of an OR, GL takes this as the reason to split that OR into cases, starting with the missing part. In the strong step for "2 ≤ n ⇒ n has a prime factorization", the scope of a factor pair p·d = n misses "2 ≤ d", and "d = 0 or d = 1 or 2 ≤ d" opens with the case "2 ≤ d", where the hypothesis can fire for d. The reason is recorded only after the scope has received the facts its opening brought, so a premise those facts already give asks for nothing. These cases are released before their induction scope settles, because the hypothesis can fire only inside them.

The recursion step also refuses to open a subproof about an object it built on top of one of those refused constructions. When the step's own goal is a fold, every accumulator guess is an existing sequence with the goal's value appended. A guess appended to the hypothesis's accumulator, or to the accumulator of a fold over a copy of a restriction, gets no subproof; a guess appended to an accumulator from the enclosing blocks still does.

A recursion step refuses a witness that links back to its own goal. Inside a recursion step every goal instruction remembers its path: the fixed arguments of the goal it came from (anchor constants excluded) and every value that has filled one of its places on the way. A witness reaches the instruction through one part of its existence; the other part may name further objects of the witness's own kind, such as the sequence a restriction is taken from. If one of those objects is on the instruction's path, compared through the equality classes, that instruction refuses the witness. The restriction C of the data prefix R to [0, rec] therefore no longer serves as the accumulator of the fold of R itself, while the restriction B of another sequence A still does. Other instructions under the same demand decide for themselves. A refused existence is parked as usual; the instructions that refused it record the refusal, so they do not wake it again, and a new instruction under the demand does.

The refusal also reads the witness's history. A witness can link back to the instruction's path without naming a path object in its existence: the facts it was derived from name it. Before an instruction admits a witness, the recursion step therefore walks the witness's derivation tree in the block's own proof history: the existence being split, the facts it was derived from, their premises in turn, one derivation per fact, as the exported proof shows it. Applied rules and goals are not followed. If a fact of that tree names an object on the instruction's path, of the witness's own kind and compared through the equality classes, the instruction refuses the witness exactly as above. Connections that a premise or the induction hypothesis forces do not count: every fact the block gets by splitting a premise, or a conclusion the induction hypothesis delivers, is listed; a parent marks such a fact when it mails it to a child, and the child lists it too. The walk stops at a listed fact, or at one equal to a listed fact through the equality classes, without checking it. Take the step goal fold(P, m, b), with path m, P, b. Its accumulator guesses D (A with b appended) and E (R with b appended) do not name P in their existences. D was derived from the facts of A, which the premise fold(P, n, a) forces, so its tree ends there and D is admitted, and so is the same append to the accumulator that the induction hypothesis forces. E was derived from the existence of R, a constructed sequence whose existence names P, and is refused. No fact in the tree of B names R, so B is still admitted to the fold of R. A witness that its existence introduces as the result of an operator, such as a sequence's value at a place, is exempt. This check is the one decision that reads the proof history (§7.8).

If neither admission route accepts the witness, the expansion is discarded after enclosing-chain and shared-expression closure. The existence remains available for demand.

4.2.2  Rejected maps hold source existences

Both rejected maps keep concrete marked demand keys and source scope. A wake sends the existence to internal mail; ordinary disintegration checks admission again. An unresolved inner existence keeps concrete outer arguments once those outer witnesses are admitted. Otherwise its enclosing existence remains parked.

Its history records zoom in for the source first, followed by its own disintegration. No expanded product list or witness-name table is retained.

4.2.4  Equivalence classes

Equality maintenance keeps two views. Application classes drive the existing expression substitutions. A separate complete view joins the scope's own classes with every visible ancestor class, including independent classes. It has its own record of changed representatives; inherited copies do not become additional application events.

An immediate existence wake first checks whether any permitted demand route could apply. Missing demand avoids building parked-source comparisons. Ancestor algebra demand and standalone operator inputs remain available. An integration demand also serves every scope below its own, so a case that can build the witness serves an integration prepared above it; inside the dry-run scope of a hypothetical disintegration only that scope's own integration demands count, and the integration admission set keeps its exact-scope rule. Every surviving source still passes its full budget, completion and history checks. Wakes deferred during equality maintenance are retained until that maintenance settles.

Name-and-scope indexes select records affected by an equality change. An unchanged component retains its cached identities. When original and normalized text are identical, one dependency walk records their shared names; future equality changes still find the same affected records. Ancestor records keep their owner while both compared forms are normalized at the descendant comparison scope. Completed construction kinds are recorded at the canonical source and scope; queued raw deliveries keep their own pending state. A separate index connects each canonical source to those deliveries. Source and demand identities finish updating before queued wakeups run; repeated wakes are combined. Replacing one value preserves other values excluded by the existing Anchor rule. Proof bridges cite inherited equalities where they were established. Compact-rule replacement keeps its existing shape eligibility even though the ownership registry also records other expansion sources.

Adding a dependency while its equality is unchanged selects only newly added or replaced records. Insertion revisions let the posting walk stop before the older records; removing a record does not recheck the survivors. A changed equality still selects every live record that depends on it.

The completed-expression check compares stored canonical identities and reads the original record's completion flag. It does not enumerate hypothetical expression variants. Literal goal names and ordinary expression multiplication retain their roles.

Comparison views are refreshed from queued changes. When a representative changes, name-and-scope postings select its affected comparisons. Newly requested views enter the same queue. A lookup rebuilds only the queued views at its target scope, in the existing expression order. It does not rescan every class on each lookup. Changes only to equality evidence retain the comparison text while the separate evidence consumers receive their required updates.

Closing a scope removes its canonical dependency records and comparison views, including ancestor records projected into that closing scope. Surviving ancestor and sibling views retain their cached forms, dependency revisions and pending updates. Private token and shape names remain allocated until discharge or canonical release. Other replay lookups retain their existing reconstruction on scope cleanup.

Watch and parked-source identities, and imported ancestor negations, are refreshed only for names whose canonical representative changed once arrivals are settled. While statement arrivals are pending, inherited-negation refresh keeps its full member list so new negations appear at the existing class-update seam. Changes to equality evidence keep settled text identities in place. Source writes are still synchronized on every refresh, including when no representative changed, and newly arriving ancestor negations keep their separate import path. Equality levels, locality and proof information retain their normal processing. An OR entry is marked stale only when the representative of a token of its canonical form, or of a token one of its spellings adds, moves, or the locality of that token's rewrite equality changes. Goal dependencies synchronize even when the representative-change list is empty.

The temporary equality-level accumulator uses an exact pair-key index to find each row. New pairs append in the same order; replacing a pair retains its row position, and existing levels keep their precedence when classes overlap. The index shares the accumulator's scratch memory and is discarded with it. Serialized classes and equality histories still come from the original ordered columns. Complete-view rebuilding remains broader work: unchanged components are currently recognized after reconstruction and history merging.

Watch dependencies select each affected expression at its own scope once across all predicate runs. The existing index also records whether an original expression is already present at that scope, avoiding repeated linear searches through long runs. Previously imported records are updated before new imports are checked. The processing order is preserved. An identifier query uses the same canonical chooser as a proof query but constructs only the expression text; proof queries still assemble their equality evidence and supporting levels.

Ancestor import applies its existing positive-statement and negated-equality exclusions before collecting and sorting candidates. Eligible negations retain the same source order, ownership and canonicalization. Shared statement indexes continue to contain every statement needed by their other consumers.

A presence check can finish at an exact source-owned key without building the other comparison entries. Admission probes take this shortcut only after the existing eligibility checks pass; otherwise they search equivalent source keys. Committed admission updates still process every matching original demand.

Admission chains distinguish fixed arguments from placeholders. Fixed arguments use the complete scoped classes; placeholders keep their binding role. If a canonical fixed name collides with a placeholder, the existing variable renaming separates them before the marker demand is created. Fixed-argument metadata survives even when that argument no longer occurs in the remaining premises.

The same binding distinction applies when existing algebra-demand values are updated or compared in a descendant scope. The source owner stays unchanged; both compared forms use the complete classes at the comparison scope.

Replay selects the argument group to mark before normalizing fixed arguments. Repeated occurrences of the same original variable remain shared. Equal fixed names cannot capture a separate placeholder or enlarge the selected marker group.

The exact sweeps and the proof checker are unchanged: the same contradictions and goals are found, and the certificate cites the same kind of rows, without keeping every negation in every spelling.

OR convergence follows statement order. Registration, equivalence products retain their existing list order when feeding OR convergence. The first applicable deposit supplies the retained contradiction witness when the history limit is reached. Repeatability requires a repeatable input sequence; it does not require an additional expression sort.

4.2.5  The revival loop — a Gauss trace

A parked rejection revives two ways: an equivalence-class rewrite (applyEquivalenceClassToRejectedMapIntegration) turns its variables into a shape that now matches an admission entry, or a new admission key arrives (revisitRejectedIntegration2) that matches the parked key. On a rewrite the hook first asks whether an admission entry for the rewritten key already exists at the park's scope: if not, the park is simply re-keyed in place under the rewritten key (every stored text rewritten, one equality1 history row per rewritten product) and waits there for a key to arrive; if it does, the rewritten compound is emitted to sameIterationInternalMail and absorbed at the top of the next cycle with full re-expansion (status=1, not the legacy mail's status=3), so the normal admission decides. The one asymmetry vs the algebra side: integration revival does not cascade through addExprToMemoryBlock (linear path only). Neither side consumes the admission entry — an admission key is a demand template, and a demand is never spent by being met, so the rule stays live for future matches (I-22).

The key match has a scope half. When the new key is below a park, both wakes also inspect its strict ancestor scopes. The product and siblings are released into the key's scope, and the ancestor park is kept. Each released expression gets one zoom in history row that cites the identical expression at the exact ancestor scope. The parent's derivation stays there; recording the transfer does not read or copy that derivation. The row also travels on the block's outgoing mail, so a descendant block that receives what is derived from the released expression can follow the citation. A product already known at the key's scope or above is not released again. A new integration key also wakes the parks in the scopes it serves: an integration demand serves its own scope and the induction scopes and OR cases reached from it through induction scopes only. There the parked source is replayed and admitted at once; the witness enters the demand's instruction, and the integration continues there, only after the induction hypothesis was used on the way. The goal's integration, prepared at the goal's scope, thus reaches the OR case that can build its witness. When a rule queues a missing premise whose open argument is uniquely determined and a statement already names that object, the premise is not integrated again. The two or-head park maps retain their exact-scope rule.

A parked existence resumes with its original depth
  1. An eligible expansion discovers a witness construction whose integration demand is missing. It stores the concrete source existence, scope, original assigned depth, supporting levels and any deferred equality steps. No permanent witness name or witness history is created yet.
  2. A later equality can re-key that source. Its original depth assignment survives. A matching demand queues the source on internal mail; an ancestor source remains available for separate descendant scopes.
  3. Absorption reconstructs the eligible construction and mints fresh witnesses at the saved depth. It does not recompute the limit from a nested source containing witnesses created during the original expansion. Source/scope receipts suppress repeated completion.
  4. History records the actual disintegration and any required equality or scope-transfer evidence. An integration-bearing result stays local as a fact; its evidence remains available to audit an exported conclusion.

4.3  Integration

Construction demand has a consumer. A supplied operator premise is recorded and unpacked. Grid startup does not replace its output with a marker to request alternative results. Explicit goals and installed rule premises, including induction hypotheses, still prepare the constructions they need.

A goal prepares only what its match determines. When a goal matches the conclusion of a rule, the rule's premises become construction demands, with the goal's objects in place of the conclusion's variables. A premise that still mentions a variable the match did not fix is left alone. Its variable is spelled like one of the block's objects, and reading it as that object would demand a construction about the wrong object. In the FTA induction steps this once turned the induction hypothesis's sequence into the block's own sequence and opened a false subproof that held three quarters of the step's statements; the same slip produced a copy of the natural numbers' set name. A premise that becomes a ≠ a is left alone too: it can never hold, so it requests no second name for a.

Integration is disintegration run backwards: reassemble a compound from its constituents so the whole can be asserted as a conclusion. The two are a matched pair — the consumer-side output-slot registration integration performs (updateAdmissionMap) is exactly what populates the admission maps disintegration consults (§4.2.1). A demand filed when a rule is installed lives at the installing rule's own scope: a rule that exists only inside a case branch demands only inside that branch (and below), never at the block's main scope — otherwise a hypothetical case's negation could open case-split cohorts at main, the loop behind a runaway burst on the D2 induction blocks before this was fixed. Reassembly uses expandSignatureForIntegration / buildIntegrationInstruction (both via the single binder rule, I-4) and is staged in a scoped child block: each step emits a validity name row (the scope it's bound to) and an expansion for integration row (the re-expansion); on completion it commits to the parent as one step. The completing step names the subproof's goal as the subproof stated it: when an equality class renamed an open goal to the class's representative witness, the record adds one equality step from the statement that closed it back to that goal. A staged integration that can't finish yet rides the same sameIterationInternalMail revival channel as a parked rejection. Three preparatory reformulation tags appear: … and, … >[bound] (carrying the bound variable), … >[] (witness slot already filled).

Equality of sets and relations. = is atomic for numbers: nothing smaller proves it. For a goal A = B whose sides are sets, GL instead integrates the two subsets: ∀x (x∈A → x∈B) and ∀x (x∈B → x∈A) (with in2 / in3 for binary and ternary relations). Each subset opens its own subproof, and once both are proved the conjunction yields A = B. Which case applies is decided on the spot when the goal arrives: the goal's logic block records the types of its variables, and a subgoal inherits its type from the definition that produced it — the implication of its subproof or the OR of its case split — following the enclosing scopes upward when that definition leaves the type open. A goal whose type cannot be found stops the run. The equality that results is the ordinary =: equivalence classes, rewriting and citations treat it like any other equality, so equal sets merge into one class. The verifier accepts such a step only when it names two genuine subset implications, and the Lean export proves it with extensionality: two sets are equal when each contains the other.

Implications inside an and wait for their plain siblings. To prove an and, GL integrates each part. An implication part is proved in its own subproof, and a subproof is expensive. When the same and also holds a plain part with fixed arguments (an atomic, negated or compound statement), the implication's subproof waits until that plain part is known at the scope or above it, up to the scope's equalities. Until then the implication is parked in rejectedMapSubproof under the first unknown plain part. Its key is the canonical text, so equal spellings share one entry. Its value is the instruction exactly as it came, because the and's own integration rule waits for that text. Every row that becomes known, and every change of a scope's equalities, is checked against the parked keys once per step; an equality change also moves a key to its new canonical text. A match reopens the parked implications as stored, or parks one again under the and's next unknown part. The and itself never waits. In the fold step of F3 this keeps both subproofs of every wrong accumulator closed; only accumulators with g(10)=14 open theirs.

Integration work belongs to no task. An admission entry, a parked implication, an installed rule, an algebra permission or an opened subproof stays until its scope closes, a class change rewrites it, or its subproof settles. An earlier design removed the work started for an expression as soon as one value of it had been built. A value position can hold several values that the block never sets equal, though: in the reverse F3 proof two accumulators of the same fold carry the values 13 and X, nothing states that the two accumulators are equal, and the removal took away the only route that built the fold with X, which the proof needs. The removal is gone, and with it that proof returns.

4.4  Branching & contradiction

Case analysis is the hardest thing GL does, and every FTA-ladder rung rests on it. An or<N> is a compiled disjunction (De Morgan: E₁∨E₂ = !(&!E₁!E₂)).

4.4.1  Splitting an OR

An OR inside an existence can be processed only after every enclosing witness is admitted. Until then it creates no OR fact, exclusion source, case or park. An unconditional case-opening signal does not bypass witness admission.

Positive logical entities of category or use on-demand exclusion and case processing. A negated conjunction with a public predicate name supplies ordinary exclusion implications at its source scope: if P = A ∧ B, then ¬P supplies A ⇒ ¬B and B ⇒ ¬A. Generated conjunctions such as and987 stay ordinary negative facts. Neither kind enters the OR source store or creates residual disjunctions or parks, and neither opens case scopes except as the premise of an implication being integrated (below).

Integration premises open their cases. When GL proves an implication by integration, the premises it places into the subproof, and the assumption ¬Dj of an OR-introduction branch (the premise of that branch's implication ¬Dj ⇒ Dk), are integration premises. Only the premise itself reacts, never an expression nested inside it. An OR premise opens its case split without any demand. A negated conjunction premise opens as the OR it denotes: ¬(a ∧ b) splits into the cases ¬a and ¬b, released one at a time in the usual order, and the verifier and the Lean export check exactly these cases. No other negated conjunction opens cases.

A conjecture's premises open their cases too. Each conjecture gets one block per premise, and the block of its last premise holds the goal. That premise is unfolded while the block is built, a moment before the goal is registered, so the block is marked as having its goal pending during that step and counts as having proof work. Every OR a premise unfolds into, including one inside its existences, opens its case split without any demand wherever there is proof work: in the premise's own block when that block holds the goal, and in every block below it that has a goal or an active contradiction. Those blocks receive the OR by mail marked “unconditional ordis”; a block below without proof work keeps it as a plain fact. Each case such a split releases is an assumption given like the premise itself, so it is opened at once with forced witnesses: a case a·y = p·x + 1 immediately yields its products a·y and p·x. For example, bez1(b,r) unfolds to coefficients x,y with bezEq(b,x,r,y) or bezEq(r,y,b,x), and both cases open. The anchor premise, shared by every conjecture, takes no part, and negated conjunctions are not split this way.

Splitting first treats every contiguous nested OR as one logical level: (D₁∨D₂)∨D₃ becomes the ordered leaf cohort D₁, D₂, D₃ in the same cycle. The original outer compiled OR remains the branch, provenance, and bookkeeping identity. Exclusion and case scheduling have separate lifecycles:

  • 1a · Exclusion on demand. An admitted OR stores one source and watches the complements of its leaves. For A ∨ B ∨ C, known ¬A produces B ∨ C; also knowing ¬B produces C. Matching shares the existing equality chooser and cached normal forms, including equality and inequality refuters. A firing records the equality justification for any refuter whose spelling changes. Only affected tokens, watched signed cores, and needed descendant scopes are revisited. A newly materialized canonical fact can wake an OR already checked in the same processing cycle. The source, facts, and equalities must have comparable scopes; the deepest participating scope receives the result.
  • Residual continuation and retirement. A firing at the source scope removes the source and all its postings. A firing below it keeps the ancestor source for other branches. A residual travels in internal mail and becomes a source after the ordinary deposit accepts it, even when ordinary disintegration is suppressed. This continuation does not grant case-opening permission. Only a selected firing records its exclusion compact and expanded rule as proof history; neither is installed as a runtime rule or known compact.
  • Exclusion remains available after case release. The original OR source and its postings stay available while its cohort processes the cases. A late complete-conjunction refutation can reduce an inherited OR in the current case. Reduction at the source's own scope retires it; reduction in a descendant keeps it available for other cases. Canonical duplicate reconciliation and owning-scope cleanup remain unchanged.
  • 1b · branch scopes, opened on demand, released one at a time. A cohort with remaining proof work opens when the firing rule carries disintegration permission, when it is an integration premise (above), or when a registered whole-compound demand names a missing disjunct. The nesting limit counts only case scopes, so a case split may open inside an OR-introduction branch but not inside another case. Algebraic requests for a disjunct's product alone do not open cases. Such requests still participate in ordinary algebra processing and can wake a parked OR for another opening check; that check still requires disintegration permission or a whole-compound demand. Opening retains that demand for every other eligible OR source while the fact is missing. A later check removes it when the fact is known at the same scope or an ancestor; a sibling case does not satisfy it. Each source still opens at most once at its parent scope. An OR one of whose disjuncts already holds at its scope (known there or at an ancestor, or literally true, such as a conjunction of reflexive equalities) is neither split into cases nor parked for a later opening: the branch of that disjunct would be the parent scope itself, so the split could only conclude what the parent already proves, and a disjunct that holds keeps holding. For the same reason an open split is retired as soon as its live or queued disjunct comes to hold at the parent scope. An open cohort queues all cases. The release step mints one branch — the disjunct named by the whole-compound demand when disintegration permission is absent, otherwise the top-ranked one: disjuncts are ordered most structurally complicated first, bare equalities after (a conjunction made only of equalities counts as an equality), equalities carrying an anchor argument dead last. Each branch scope (…_ordis_…, interned by the deposit door from its seed) opens with D_i asserted as its seed (delivered through internal mail, so a compound disjunct decomposes like any statement); the remaining disjuncts wait in a pending queue. The next branch releases only when the live one resolves — it derives one of its relevant goals, or its asserted disjunct is refuted and the branch retires. A resolved branch does not keep working: its relevant goals are those of the closest scope above it that holds goals. Walking up from the case, the first scope with goals defines the set, and goals further up are ignored: inside a subproof a case can only help close the subproof's goals, because the subproof exports nothing but its own result. If such a case derives the negation of its subproof's goal, its cohort can never converge, and the whole subproof is abandoned (nothing is proved or refuted by that). Goals live only at main, in subproofs and in OR-integration scopes, never in a case. A block primed for contradiction keeps the contradiction as an implicit goal of main, which a closer goal shadows like any other. An inherited OR received from an ancestor can enter this same processing when the recipient has an explicit goal or active contradiction work. It keeps its external status; demand and depth still control case opening. Selected exclusions cite their compact implications in proof history, keeping the full proof chain. Active contradiction blocks keep cases with no explicit goals working toward contradiction. Otherwise, at Phase 3 entry, a branch with no such goals, or with all relevant goals already known at the branch or above, is excluded from further inference together with its descendants. This decision is fixed for the phase; live siblings and parent-scope processing continue. History is still absorbed and emitted. The shared closure sweep at the end of phases 1 and 3 queues resolved subtrees for wipeout. Phase-1 closure finishes before GPU request construction. Runtime facts, classes, rules and local subgoals are removed; booked case contributions and proof history stay. Goals above the branch close when the case conclusions converge at their parent scope. Only the derivation-level sets of currently proved goals are kept separately, so a later sibling refutation can finish their case convergence after the runtime rows are gone. A refuted case keeps the level set of its refutation the same way. And a logic block with neither explicit goals nor active contradiction work opens, parks and releases no or branch. Demand-gated opening, common-case waiting and the unfired-induction gate limit case growth. The other disjuncts' negations are not seeded in _ordis_; the original source remains available for late exclusion. Independent sources at other parent scopes retain their own eligibility. The OR nesting cap (max_or_depth) still bounds branch-scope depth; exclusion matching remains eligible at every depth.
  • One execution of a common case at a scope. If cohort A is running X and cohort B still contains X, all of B waits, even when B ranks Y above X. Once X finishes, B reuses its goal results and continues its remaining cases. Only equalities visible at the common parent can identify cases. Different parent scopes remain independent. Cases opened as distinct assumptions are not combined retroactively if a later equality identifies them. A stalled X can therefore hold back several cohorts.
  • Each cohort keeps its own proof and premise use. Reusing X creates no second inference subtree. Its derivation is recorded under B's case scope, and its case assumption cites B's OR. When the assumptions differ, the proof cites the directed parent equalities for the changed arguments only. Identical arguments add no equality premise credit. The hypothetical case carries the levels of the ordinary facts it actually uses; each cohort adds its own OR-source levels when its cases converge. Thus sharing does not falsely credit A's OR premise in B's proof.
  • Wait for an induction application. No OR case opens below an explicit induction subscope before its hypothesis fires. Cases remain queued. When the induction tree settles, the winning scope remains and its cases become eligible on the next burst. Cases at the induction block's main scope remain eligible.
  • 0 · one OR processing representative per scope. Upstream of 1a and 1b, at the deposit door itself: when an or<N> statement arrives whose equivalence class already has a fully-processed representative at that scope — same operator and equality-canonical form, also allowing a compiled interchangeable digit pair — the newcomer stays a passive statement. It registers and is multiplied by the equivalence classes like any fact, and nothing else happens: no rule emission, no cohort, no parking, on every arrival path. Each scope keeps a small ledger of the or statements it has fully processed; each entry carries the or's canonical processing identity next to its original spelling and is re-keyed whenever an equality changes the classes at that scope, so the probe is a single lookup that stays correct as the classes grow. Paths that deliberately re-run an or's consumption (a parked or's revival, an equivalence-class rewrite of a parked or) clear the ledger row first. Two cohorts that opened before their spellings became equal are collapsed at the end of the burst: the one with the most statements already derived under its branch scopes survives, the others stop scheduling and their live branches are wiped — completed case records and all history stay, and anything removed re-derives under the survivor. Without this gate, or processing was self-feeding: every new equi spelling of one or got the full consumption, which minted statements and equalities, which minted more spellings.

Symmetry is checked once from the definition. A numbered OR with exactly two digit-valued parameters is marked when swapping them preserves its signed disjunct multiset. Equality may reverse its arguments; other predicates keep theirs ordered. For trichotomy, a < b, a = b, b < a are the same alternatives after exchanging a and b. Free parameters, binding and repeated disjuncts are preserved.

Scope and proof boundary: late evidence must be visible at the reduction scope. A sibling case cannot supply it. The existing selected-exclusion proof records the source OR and the actual refuters. Residuals continue through ordinary admission and can coexist with the original cohort; the case-depth limit and branch-resolution rules remain unchanged.

Lean replays a residual deduction by assuming that residual is false, reconstructing each surviving alternative, and contradicting the original OR together with the recorded refuters. Every original alternative is accounted for with its exact arguments and sign.

This replay applies equally to two or more alternatives: each binary step preserves the original grouping, and a negated alternative retains its polarity.

Exclusion first selects compatible refuters and their scopes. A separate materialization step consumes those choices immediately, applies the remaining scope restrictions, and constructs the checked proof history and internal mail. Selection itself produces no facts or source retirement.

Nested ORs open in one go. A compiled OR child is never stored as an opaque branch in either direction. Both _ordis_ and _orint_ recursively expand OR children until the leaves are non-OR expressions, preserve their order, and keep every leaf under the outer OR signature. Flattening stops at any non-OR operator, so it changes only disjunction syntax, not other definitions.

1c · The introduction half. The exclusion sources of 1a consume a disjunction that is already known; nothing above produces one from a single true disjunct. That half emits at a different site: when the prover installs a compiled implication whose premise is a disjunction (for example an enumeration description's membership-intro conjunct “x=a ∨ x=b ∨ x=c ⇒ x∈M”), it also installs one intro rule per flattened leaf that contains every open argument of the disjunction (a leaf of a closed theorem's OR premise may miss one, and then gets none), D_k → (D₁∨…∨D_K) — propositionally valid unconditionally, a flat rule with no scopes. A single concretely-true disjunct — typically reached through an equality witness alias, since the true disjunct at a concrete element is reflexive — can then state the disjunction as a fact and fire the implication's own rule. This closed the rung-2.1 membership gap: the enumerated set's concrete memberships were underivable because the disjunction statement at a concrete element had no producer. The same intro rules, compiled with the goal's definition, are installed once per scope when a goal is flattened and one of its premises is a disjunction: a case branch that proves one disjunct can then state the goal's disjunction and fire the flattened goal rule (FTA row 121). When a disjunct is itself an existence, it also gets one rule that builds it from its own body, so a disjunct whose witnesses already exist is derived directly from the facts (G3's base case: 1·1 = 0·1 + 1 from the facts about 1 and 0). This addition goes one level deep only.

4.4.3  Full contradiction discharge

A full contradiction belongs to the current logic block, not to one conditional OR branch. The prover discharges only when both X and !X exist at that block's exact main scope. A copy may also exist deeper; the separate main row still qualifies. primedForContradiction keeps the block alive long enough to emit its stored contradictionTheoremId. The discharge fires at most once per block per step, gated on isActive (I-78).

A block with no special role that reaches such a pair has proved its own premise set inconsistent: it is flagged and deactivated together with its whole subtree, every theorem produced at or below it is suppressed at emission, and rows it already submitted in earlier cycles are retracted from the global theorem list — nothing vacuous ships. After each batch a vacuity pass finishes the job: theorems whose premise sets are certified unsatisfiable (two proved theorems with the same premises and contradictory heads), and theorems whose proofs cite any vacuous theorem, are classified out of the deliverable pool into a dedicated artifact — their chapters stay for audit, but they never feed a later batch. A genuine reductio is unaffected, because only the contradiction child holds the assumed seed, so its parent never flags. The same pair at an anchor block stops the run: it would mean the definition set itself is inconsistent.

4.4.4  A contradiction scope looks for its contradiction

To prove a negated compound, the prover assumes the compound in a contradiction scope and waits for a statement there to meet its negation. Waiting alone can stall: a rule whose conclusion would be that negation fires only when all its premises are known, and nothing asks for a compound premise that was never built. Goals ask for their premises through the necessity check; a contradiction scope now asks the same way. For every fact the scope sees, it looks for the rules whose conclusion is the fact's negation. When such a rule's premises are fully determined by that conclusion, all known but one, and the missing one is a conjunction, the scope integrates that conjunction itself.

Two checks keep the demand precise. The conjunction's plain parts must already hold, and at least one of them inside the scope itself, so the contradiction the rule would close is the scope's own and not one that was available before it opened. A candidate that still waits for a premise or a plain part is parked and woken when that statement arrives, like a parked subproof. The conjunction's implication part is proved in a subproof opened inside the contradiction scope, and its result stays in that scope: a subproof's result always lands in the scope that opened it, because its proof may use that scope's assumption. The work follows change only: a new scope is matched once against every rule, a new fact against the rules the scopes have seen, and a new rule against an index of the facts under the conclusions that would negate them. Replayed offline on the recorded state of the well-ordering lemma's stalled step, where the scope sees about two thousand facts and fifteen hundred rules, six and a half thousand conclusion matches leave fifteen candidates to decide and two conjunctions that qualify, one of them the minimum the step needs.

4.4.5  OR-theorem construction

A theorem whose head is an OR is not proved directly. After the prove pass, headSwitchOne populates orPairsFromHeadSwitch; run_modes::fullRun pairs mutually-exclusive parents (!D_a→D_b with !D_b→D_a) and constructOrTheorem assembles the or<N> theorem. The disjunct set is canonicalised (min,max) to dedup the symmetric pair; the two parents are marked consumed and moved to compressed_out_theorems.txt — the OR subsumes them. A single proved direction already licenses the OR, and the run builds it as soon as that direction is proved; a companion proved afterwards is not registered, since the OR stands for it and its proof may rest on the OR. The companion's own logic blocks therefore stop at that moment: its goal is closed as settled, the same way a contradiction twin closes a goal it proved, and its contradiction and induction blocks are deactivated.

4.5  Induction

Induction is scheduled on a structurally-identified (1)-typed bound variable n, once per enabled kind. The candidates are the theorem's natural-number variables that are always inputs: a variable that sits even once at an output slot, or at a slot its predicate lists as neither input nor output, is never one, and neither is an anchor argument. A prime factor sequence P of length k and product n takes k and P as inputs and n as its output, so a theorem about it inducts on k, never on n. Two sub-goal blocks are spawned under the conjecture's goal block: base (n := 0, parked until the step succeeds) and step (seeds s(rec) = n and installs the induction hypothesis as a rule). The kinds differ only in that hypothesis. Weak induction assumes the head at rec, with the other digit arguments fixed. A weak hypothesis whose premises each leave two arguments open (two prime factor sequences P', Q' of length rec with a common product n') cannot start using a premise; GL then asks for such a premise with both open arguments marked. Each open argument is a path: every key names one of them as the marker, and a part that holds both keys both paths, the other argument matching any value. The prefix of the given sequence that an existence offers can start the integration, or a product can, and the other argument is filled afterwards. When two premises of the hypothesis ask for the same goal, as P' and Q' do, each gets its own copy of the goal's instructions, bound to the conjecture premise it came from: the copy for P refuses an arriving object that names Q or a witness forced from Q's premise, and the copy for Q the reverse, so P' is built from P and Q' from Q. Strong induction assumes the head at every m < n, written m + x = n and m ≠ n, with every variable except n and the anchor arguments free, so the step may use it at a smaller value with different other arguments. It is built only for a lemma that speaks of n in its premises as a property of n alone, as a relation to another induction variable, or as a set fixed by n (the set of its divisors), and that holds no operator computing a number or a sequence: a smaller value gets no companion for a sequence, a product or an element of a set. A lemma may have one such variable, or two whose exchange leaves its premises as they are (coprime(a, b) cannot tell a and b apart; a ∈ ℕ and 1 ≤ b can), read through a symmetric flag every relation carries in the configuration; with three or more, or two the premises tell apart, it gets none. Every other lemma gets weak induction only. A catch, the existence that supplies the smaller value, must compute it from the lemma's induction variables, as the remainder of a divided by b or a cofactor of n; an existence over a constant, such as the remainder of 2 divided by n, is not caught. The strong step also asks for such a value: it files m + x = n, with m and x open, as an integration demand, so a value below n that an existence offers (a remainder of a division by n) can be admitted and the hypothesis fires at it. Beside it the strong step installs a second hypothesis, the multiplication catcher: the same head at m behind x · m = n and m ≠ n (n is a successor, so x ≥ 1 and again m < n). Its demand catches m as the second factor: the cofactor d of a divisor p, with p · d = n. Like every induction admission, both demands are gated: it serves only an expression whose arguments are the conjecture's input values (for G3, a and b) and its sets and operators. An expression over rec, over a value the proof constructed, or over a constant the conjecture uses only as a parameter (G3: 0, 1, 2) is refused, so the demand does not multiply over its own products. The hypothesis's own firings are gated the same way. A shot whose premise is the conclusion of an earlier shot is dropped before it reaches the block. So is a shot whose premise derives from such a conclusion through any number of steps: a remainder or quotient the shot produced, an object built on them, or a fact about them. The hypothesis never shoots at what its own shots made. When both blocks land, the theorem is promoted only if (in[n,N]) is already known at the conjecture's own block: without that typing fact, induction would range over all entities, not the naturals — unsound (I-18). The method is induction or strong induction (induction variable in the reference column); the chapter is the triad induction_typing + check_zero + check_induction_condition.

4.5.1  Steps whose hypothesis can only repeat a premise

Some weak steps cannot gain anything from their hypothesis. Row 81 of the FTA pool says fold(P, s(n)) = fold(P, n) · P(s(n)). Its hypothesis, applied at s(rec) = n, says fold(Q, n) = fold(Q, rec) · Q(n); in the best case Q is P, and fold(P, n) is already a premise. Since a fold has one value, the hypothesis can only repeat that premise. The step block nevertheless built sequences and folds for it and was the slowest block of the batch, while the conjecture's own block proves row 81 directly.

GL now tests such a step in a scope is_useful on the step block's main. The test applies when the hypothesis head is an operator whose output the configuration declares unique (uniquely_definable, the user's hint) and the conjecture has exactly one premise with that operator. At grid build GL plants there the hypothesis premises under their own names and the head with its output replaced by a test variable test_level_<level>_<serial>. The scope sees everything main knows, so main's own rules fire on the planted rows and write their results into the scope, never into main; equalities form classes there as anywhere. Nothing is disintegrated, admitted or made a goal there. No new rule is needed: the Peano anchor already says that a function has one value, so the planted s(rec) = 10 and the seed s(rec) = 9 give 10 = 9 in the scope. After each round of standard processing GL reads the head through the scope's classes: it is fold(P, 9) = ?. The key is the conjecture's premise fold(P, 9) = a with its output marked; when it matches the head everywhere but that slot, the barrier at the end of the burst switches the step block off. Row 81's step is caught in the third burst: the rule needs to know that rec is a natural number, which reaches main at the end of the second. A test that has not caught its head after the batch's third burst is closed. Either way the scope is wiped. The test only prunes: a wrong hint can cost an induction route, never add a fact.

4.5.2  Twin premises: one induction, a free partner

The full uniqueness of prime factorizations reads: if P is a prime factor sequence of n with length k and Q one of n with length l, then P = Q. Its two premises use the same predicate, and exchanging them gives the same statement once the variables are renumbered in order of appearance (the equality P = Q reads either way). Such premises are twins, and l, the variable the second twin holds where the first holds k, is the partner of k. An induction on l would only mirror the induction on k, so GL builds the triad on k alone. The step needs the hypothesis at the predecessors of both lengths, so the weak hypothesis does not fix l: it holds for every length of the second sequence. The weak step block and the zero blocks remember the partner for the case split on it (4.5.3).

4.5.3  The case experiment on the partner

In the step of the full uniqueness proof, the hypothesis is needed at the predecessor q of the partner l. The partner is a natural number, so it is zero or the successor of some q, and the step block takes the two cases one after the other. It assumes the successor case first: there is a q with s(q) = l, and q is a fact inside that case, where the second integration can use it and the step is proved. Then it assumes l = 0, which cannot hold: a sequence of length 0 has a single entry, so n would be a prime, while the first sequence has at least two prime entries. Refuting that case needs the results of the first integration, which by then have reached the block. With both cases closed, the step holds in the block. A zero block runs the mirrored experiment: it assumes only the successor case, refutes it, and obtains l = 0. The refutation needs the last entry x of the second sequence: x is smaller than n, as one of at least two prime factors of n, and equal to n, as a prime dividing the prime n. GL knows that this entry exists, but the fact stays parked while nothing in the block asks for it. So when the successor case opens, the zero block asks for the partner's entry there, and the parked fact is opened inside the case.

4.5.4  Two premises, one integration group

The hypothesis of the step is applied to two facts: a prime factor sequence of length rec (the first sequence with its last prime removed) and one of length q (the second sequence shortened the same way), both for the same product. Both have to be built by integration, and neither may use the other's objects. GL starts the first integration when the hypothesis is installed and the second as soon as q appears in the successor case of the case experiment. There GL first names q rec2 with a copy q = rec2, the same way the predecessor of the induction variable is called rec, and the second goal is the first one with rec2 in place of rec. Each runs in its own int scopes, bound to its own premise, with rec2 playing rec's part for the second. The two form one group: once both are finished, the first one's results reach the block and the second one's the case, at the same moment, as for two identical goals.

4.6.1  Why batches 4 and 5 widen the gate

The locality gate — “at least one matched premise must be a fact this LB owns locally” — is a defence against the engine drowning in cross-product work. But when an ancestor scope mails in a new fact, or a freshly-installed local rule pairs against pre-existing inherited facts, the locality gate would silently withhold the obvious fire. Batches 4 and 5 are the exceptions: they relax the gate precisely once per cycle, and they cap the input pool to keep the relaxation cheap.

4.6.2  What happens if batch 5 is missing

A rule installed in cycle N would not see ancestor-inherited facts until cycle N+1 (batch 1's locality requirement misses the rule's freshness). The practical effect: a one-cycle delay across every rule installation. For a deep induction triad that installs and consumes rules in sequence, the delay multiplies — the run effectively becomes deeper × deeper. Batch 5 collapses that to one cycle.

4.7  Admission closure before publication

Admitting an inner witness also admits the enclosing witnesses that have a place in the matched goal at or above the matched part, or beside it; an inner witness whose enclosing witness has no such place waits for it and follows once it is admitted. The shared-expression cascade coadmits dependent witnesses until the selected set stops growing. Permanent names are assigned only to admitted witnesses. Publication and OR processing wait for this closure; unresolved expansions are discarded and their source existences are parked.

4.7.1  Closure and later demand

Closure resolves witness dependencies within the current expansion. A later demand finds the parked existence and sends it through internal mail for fresh expansion and admission. Successful source/scope receipts suppress repeated minting, while unresolved nested sources remain eligible.

Pure premises before a new construction

Ordinary admission requires pure premises in every scope, including an OR branch. An iteration-bearing argument is pure only when it is a registered product of recursion. An algebraic witness can still participate in ordinary inference, but cannot demand another construction through an ordinary marker. The separate integration demand for a missing compound premise keeps its justification-specific exception.

A supplied conjecture must be proved before later batches can use it. For example, successor cancellation can derive a sum equal to an existing value directly, avoiding an intermediate sum witness in an enumeration interval proof.

Induction witnesses and flat integration

The induction hypothesis is unpacked through the integration witness path without waiting for demand. Its defining facts can then satisfy a flat integration rule: one additional rule joins the nested definition's premises into the complete goal. Existing step-by-step integration remains available.

Generated conjunction and existence wrappers open automatically. Each configured conjunction or existence compound can remain whole or open one level, producing distinct premise combinations with correctly bound witnesses. The verifier checks the construction; Lean reconstructs the goal from the cited premises.

Each nested choice retains its own immutable return point. Exploring a later sibling cannot change where an earlier choice resumes: every combination includes every required sibling, even when both contain further choices. A shortened premise list is not a definition construction and is rejected.

Integration demand rules require at least six non-anchor premises and may use the hypothesis's iteration witnesses. Other demand rules retain their existing purity requirement and four-premise threshold. A fact already known in the current scope or an ancestor creates no missing-premise demand; a stale demand opens no branch.

Delivering the common branch result

When the final OR branch supplies a conclusion during mail absorption, the parent proof goes to the output channel together with its history. Clearing the consumed input therefore leaves the new proof available for the next drain. Both branch derivations remain the evidence for convergence.

Before the burst, output is the same-iteration channel. After the burst, output is the next-iteration channel. Convergence outside mail absorption keeps its established delivery schedule.

Firing direct supporting rules

For a singleton relation containing only (0,n), a direct rule requires both x=n and i unequal to 0, then concludes that (i,x) is not in the relation. A second rule uses x unequal to n and i=0. Neither route first creates the negative equality conjunction.

The original contrapositive remains available when its negative conjunction premise is obtained independently. Every direct rule still requires all its guards. The compiler uses the existing single-premise consumer boundary; other owning implications keep their guarded helpers.

Forced witnesses and integration depth

Assumptions and the existing forced routes introduce force_lev_… witnesses. Ordinary integration creates int_<depth>_lev_… witnesses. The original outer expression assigns one depth to the entire expansion: zero when it contains no integration witness, otherwise one more than the deepest integration witness it contains. Forced and algebraic names do not contribute.

When a block proves two of its names equal, it merges them into one class and mails the equality to the blocks below it, which merge the same names; a fact that mentions a merged name other than the class's representative stays home, and only its history travels.

Circulating facts and compact rules containing an ordinary integration witness stay in their owning logic block. Forced witnesses may travel under the usual scope and routing rules. Internal replay and proof-history transport remain available. Canonical names prefer ordinary values, then forced witnesses, then integration witnesses by numeric depth, then algebraic witnesses; lexical order breaks remaining ties.

A replay created while an input mailbox is being read goes to the output mailbox with its saved depth and proof history. Clearing the consumed input therefore preserves the newly queued construction.

Trial scopes for candidate integration witnesses

An integration instruction can require a fixed fact of the witness it admits. For example, a candidate accumulator sequence B must have the value p at the endpoint m. Such a requirement is a gate: a non-implication element of a conjunction on a side of the integrated existence, whose arguments are all fixed once the candidate replaces the marker. The fact the candidate was matched by is never its gate, since every candidate satisfies it. Every new name one admission creates is checked in the same way: the matched witness, the enclosing witnesses it brings along and the witnesses selected with them on the other side of the instruction. Each takes on the requirement of the role it fills in the instruction. An appended sequence, for example, enters along with its domain interval and fills the accumulator, so it takes on the value p at m. When every admitting route of a source is gated, all the new names are minted in a trial scope of their own, below the scope that disintegrated the source and named after them. There they take part only in rule firings, equality classes and structural expansion. No new witness, case split, subproof, integration or goal is created there. The trial records the witnesses it minted and the facts it published.

When the gate elements of one instruction are known in the trial scope, the trial passes. The source is replayed in the parent scope, which admits the recorded witnesses under the names the trial minted, with the recorded facts, and the instruction's subproofs open; the trial scope is wiped. A candidate whose requirement never holds stays in its trial scope, so its consequences never reach the parent. A witness with an ungated route is admitted as before.

One scope for each use of the induction hypothesis

An induction step block uses its hypothesis by eating it forward: a premise is filled, and a witness that fills the next premise is admitted from an existence the block knows. Each such admission opens its own induction scope below the scope where the existence was offered. The admitted witnesses, their facts and the hypothesis chain advanced by the admission live only there, and the next admission of the same chain opens a scope inside it: the eating moves forward, one order per path. Once the hypothesis has eaten one place at a scope, it eats only that place there — a further witness for the same place opens a sibling scope, a parallel path — and every other place is eaten inside the child scopes; an existence for another place that arrives at the parent is mailed into the children. Two parallel paths never see each other's facts: the work of the block grows with the number of paths, not with their product. A human proof uses the hypothesis the same way, along one line, without combining parallel attempts.

One unfolding can offer both strong demands a witness: for the least divisor p of n, the fact p ≤ n unfolds to p + x = n and p | n to p · d = n. Each catch opens its own scope, and each scope gets its own copy of the facts on the path down to its catch: both scopes hold the facts of the divisor set and of p, each holds only its own catch. The two scopes stay independent: neither sees the other's catch.

A scope is named by the chain of the key that admitted, with the admitted place written as a marker, and by the one or two names the key admitted; the strong hypothesis's demand for a smaller value marks its two places marker1 and marker2. An admission whose chain already named a scope on its path is refused, so a chain only moves forward and the strong hypothesis takes one smaller value per path. Its two catches, the sum m + x = n and the product x · m = n, share that limit: inside a scope either of them opened, neither catches again, so the case analysis one catch starts is never repeated with fresh objects inside the other catch's scope. Only the hypothesis's own chain opens scopes; the step's other demands, such as its goal's, admit as before. The scopes carry no assumption: a statement that equals a goal visible at the scope tree's root is placed at the root in the same step, where that goal is handled as usual; nothing else leaves. When the hypothesis fires in one leaf, its tree settles in that step: every other scope of the tree is wiped, the firing leaf and the scopes above it stay and the work continues there, and the hypothesis opens no further scope in that tree - it is used once, along one line. A scope whose admitted names all turn out to be objects the root already has (the smaller value is found to be 0 and its distance the step's own variable, for example) is wiped as well: it would only repeat what the root holds. When an object the root already has fills the hypothesis directly, the root keeps that step, and the use also opens its own scope named by the object; this scope carries the line its aliases repeat and is never wiped. The raw proof graph keeps the scopes for audit; the processed proof graph, which the verifier, the Lean export and the book read, deletes them, so readers see one linear proof in the step's scope.

One scope for each premise's integration

One step block can need the same integration twice. The step of the factorization theorem holds two prime-factor sequences, and its hypothesis demands the same integration goal for each of them. A shared pool of facts usually helps integration, because one fact can shorten several subproofs; here the two integrations must have nothing to do with each other. Each copy of the goal is therefore bound to its premise, filed only under keys that hold the induction variable or rec, and started only from an existence whose arguments are numbers or rec. Its first start opens an integration scope below the scope where the existence was offered, named by the premise and the starting witness. The witness's facts and the rest of that integration live only there, and no integration scope opens inside another. A scope is finished when one of its statements is the integration goal; for each premise only its first finished scope counts. When every premise has one, those scopes are promoted to the parent at the same time, every statement they hold registered there so that the parent can relate the two results, and then wiped together with every other integration scope of the same goal, which can no longer contribute; the goal opens no further scope. Proof processing removes the scopes, so a reader sees the promoted statements derived at the parent.

Where an induction step applies its hypothesis, every integration key must be tied to the induction, not only the first keys of the two copies: everywhere in a weak step, whose hypothesis at rec is installed in its main scope, and in a strong step inside the scopes its hypothesis opens for a caught smaller value, while the strong step's own goal files its keys in its main scope as in any block. A key is filed there only when one of its arguments is rec, the induction variable n, the number before rec or the number after n (known as successor facts at the scope), an object a tied key of the step admitted, or, in a strong step, the smaller value its hypothesis caught or that value's distance to n. Such a size no longer counts where the scope knows it is zero: a size of zero admits every one-element object, and the batch has one for every number. Such an object carries its size: an interval admitted with the length rec lets a later key that names the interval be filed, while a key such as "an ordered sequence", which names nothing of the step's size, is never filed, so a one-element sequence cannot start a candidate. The keys of a strong step's own goal stay filed in its main scope, because the scopes its hypothesis opens read them; outside those scopes, though, a key that names nothing of the step's size admits no object. With FTA run as one batch, such a key had admitted a one-element sequence for each of six numbers into the main scope of I4's strong step, and their prime-sequence subproofs held nine tenths of the block.

Every other block plays the same game on the goal's own variables. The goal's head variables are its arguments that are not constants shared by the whole batch, such as n in “every n from 2 on has a prime factorization”. A key is filed there only when it names a head variable, or an object that such a key admitted in the same block, or repeats a shared constant exactly as often as the goal does, more than once: a goal about the interval from 0 to 0 may look for that interval; a goal whose arguments are all shared constants files every key. In the goal block of that theorem, both first keys, “an ordered sequence” and “a sequence of primes”, name only constants, so neither is filed: no one-element sequence, not even that of n, starts a candidate there, and the theorem is proved through its strong step. With FTA run as one batch, five such sequences had filled 95% of that block.

Sums, products and order facts built on demand

The anchor states that every two numbers have a sum, and likewise a product. Each statement is a rule with two typing premises and an existence conclusion. Fired eagerly, it creates a sum and a product for every pair of numbers a block knows, although a proof uses only the few that a goal or an integration step asks for. Such a rule is recognised by its shape when a block installs it: its premises only type distinct variables in anchor sets, and its conclusion is an existence defined by one application of an anchor operator with the witness as the result. The block records that it holds the rule but does not add it to hash memory.

When a demand for one value appears, the prover builds that one existence exactly as the firing would have produced it: same scope, levels, generation and proof history. A demand that arrived before the rule or before its inputs were typed is served once per burst, as soon as the missing part is there. No other rule may use such an existence as a premise; installation checks this.

The anchor's order theorems work the same way. Any two numbers are comparable (a ≤ b or b ≤ a), and exactly one of a < b, a = b, b < a holds. Each of these theorems is a rule with two typing premises and an OR conclusion. Fired eagerly, it creates an OR for every pair of numbers, although a proof uses one only to rule out a disjunct once the other is known to fail. Such a rule is recognised by its shape and held without entering hash memory. Its OR is built for one pair when that OR could do something: a fact refutes one of its disjuncts (for a < b this includes a fact that a ≤ b fails, or that a = b), a case split on a disjunct is demanded, or the OR itself is a goal.

A refuting fact whose numbers are not typed yet waits until the typing arrives. A rule that reaches a block late picks up the refuting facts and goals already there, and a stored case demand is offered again once per burst.
